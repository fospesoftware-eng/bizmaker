MARKET INTELLIGENCE — AUTUMN 2025

Where capital is moving in the UAE real-asset market.

The market is not short of capital. It is short of assets that can survive a more demanding underwriting standard — and that distinction is now setting prices.

For most of the past cycle, the UAE real-estate conversation was about access. Could capital get into the market, at what yield, and how quickly could it be deployed? That question has changed. Capital is now asking a harder one: what happens to this asset when the assumptions behind it move?

The bid has narrowed

We see a wider gap between the assets that attract competitive bids and those that sit. The dividing line is rarely the headline quality of the building. It is the clarity of the income, the credibility of the business plan and the number of buyers who can underwrite the same story.

Prime offices in established districts continue to draw institutional attention where the lease profile is long and the tenant base is diversified. Secondary stock with imminent rollover, deferred maintenance or a single dominant tenant is being priced for that risk — often more severely than owners expect.

The same pattern appears in residential. Well-located, well-managed product clears. Product that relies on a generic handover story and an optimistic absorption curve does not.

The question is no longer whether capital is interested in the UAE. It is whether the asset can carry the structure the capital needs.

Income quality is the story

Headline yield still opens the conversation, but it no longer closes it. Underwriters are spending more time on the components beneath it: covenant strength, lease length, escalation, service-charge recovery, reinstatement obligations and the cost of retaining a tenant at renewal.

This is especially visible in commercial assets. A building let at a strong rent to a tenant with a short lease and expensive fit-out is not the same asset as one let slightly below market on a longer covenant. The difference shows up in the exit, not only the entry.

We increasingly model a re-letting cost alongside the rent roll: the incentive, downtime and capital expenditure required to keep the income in place. Owners who have not priced that cost often overestimate the durability of their yield.

Platforms over one-offs

A second shift is structural. Capital that once bought a single building is now looking for platforms — a logistics portfolio, a hospitality operating business, a data centre campus with expansion capacity, a residential rental platform with management in place.

The appeal is not simply scale. A platform gives the investor a repeatable route to deploy further capital, a management team already in position and a clearer exit to a buyer who wants the same capabilities. It also changes the diligence: the operating model matters as much as the real estate.

For owners, this creates an opportunity to aggregate. Individual assets that are unremarkable on their own can become part of a platform that commands a different buyer pool. It also raises the bar: an incomplete portfolio with inconsistent documentation is harder to sell than the sum of its parts suggests.

Infrastructure is pulling capital

The third shift is geographic and thematic. Capital is following infrastructure — logistics corridors, aviation and port nodes, power capacity, fibre routes and rail. The question is no longer only “where is the demand?” but “where is the enabling infrastructure, and when will it be available?”

This is most obvious in digital infrastructure. A data centre site with a secured power allocation and a credible fibre route is a different proposition from land with an aspiration. Investors are learning to price the connection before the building.

The same logic applies to land. Infrastructure announcements move values before planning catches up. The opportunity is not in reacting to the announcement; it is in reading the sequence of delivery and underwriting the timing risk.

What this means for owners

  • Prepare the evidence, not only the brochure. A data room that answers the covenant, capex and exit questions shortens the bid process.
  • Price the rollover. Assume the tenant asks for something at renewal. Build that into the hold case before a buyer does.
  • Think about the buyer at exit. The price you can achieve today is constrained by the universe of buyers who can underwrite the asset later.
  • Consider aggregation. If your asset is sub-scale alone, a platform may be the better route to a competitive process.

What we are watching

Three things will tell us whether this selectivity deepens or eases: the pace of new prime supply in established districts; the cost and availability of debt for income assets; and the speed at which power and connectivity are delivered to the next wave of strategic sites.

None of these is a simple bet on the market. They are the variables that determine which assets can carry a credible capital structure — and which will need a different owner, a different use or a different price.
